Does the pressure model know which balls matter?

I learned how much each ball's situation weighs from the data: weighted ball values must predict who wins the match. Lower log-loss is better. The learned weights beat both treating every ball equally and my first hand-set weights, in 5-fold cross-validation by match.

How stable are the lists?

Each row changes one setting and re-runs the ratings. The big number is how many of the ten available players stay the same; the small one is the rank correlation of the whole list with the final version. Darker cells are more stable. The high-pressure cut-off moves the lists most; international and A-level batting are the least stable lists.

How the lists are built, and what to keep in mind
  • Pressure is the extra need for a good performance. Each ball's pressure mixes consequence (65%: how much the ball moves the result, learned by a small neural network) with difficulty (35%: how hard the situation is for this batter or bowler, from the required rate, a bowler's previous over, a new batter after quick wickets and so on). It is then scaled by match stakes: playoff and must-win games count more, and dead games almost nothing. In the IPL, each team's chances of reaching the top four and top two come from 4,000 simulations of the rest of the league, with ties on points broken by net run rate (the real one to date, plus margins of real IPL games for the rest). The top 25% of balls in each competition are high-pressure (HP) balls.
  • Dots, wickets and boundaries all count, at their value in that situation: a dot ball earns the bowler the runs an average bowler would concede there (about 1.5 on high-pressure balls), and a boundary earns the batter the runs above what was expected (about 3). In a chase, a wicket costs the batting side the drop in its chance of winning, turned into runs at that moment's rate; in a first innings it costs the runs the side is expected to lose. The tables also show the raw counts.
  • Wicket luck: wickets and dismissals are rare, so each player's count is pulled toward what an average player would get on the same balls, by an amount the data sets (how much players really differ, beyond chance). For bowlers on high-pressure balls the data finds no difference beyond chance, so wicket hauls above or below expected don't move their ratings; their skill shows through runs and dot balls. Batters' dismissal counts do differ for real and are pulled in only partly.
  • Performance is runs and wickets against what an average player would get in the same situation, weighted by competition (IPL and full-member T20Is 1.0, A-teams 0.7, associate T20Is 0.5) and recency (0.85 per year before 2026), and shrunk towards average when the sample is small.
  • Eligibility: IPL and international lists need 60 HP balls (10 overs) in at least 5 matches; A-level lists need 20 HP balls in at least 2 matches.
  • Super overs are treated as the most extreme pressure. They change who is in the IPL top 10, but not which available players make the list.
  • Availability uses the IPL 2026 squads in the data pack, the trades, and the release lists all ten teams announced (from media reports; KKR's list differs between two reports). Players released by their team count as available even before they register for the auction. Pakistani players, players retired from the IPL, banned players, those who opted out of 2027 (Head, Starc), and IPL or international players not seen since 2024 are not available. Bangladesh players and those who skipped IPL 2026 (Maxwell, Moeen Ali, Faf du Plessis) are flagged.
  • Reliability: pressure samples are small, so a top-10 chance of 30–50% is normal. Read close ranks as ties. The data pack has no domestic cricket and no capped or uncapped field.
